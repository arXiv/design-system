    Abstract

    We investigate the scalar sector of linear cosmological perturbations in quadratic gravity. Working in the Einstein frame, we derive the equations of motion in a gauge-independent manner and express them in terms of three sets of gauge-invariant variables. This approach allows us to distinguish genuine physical effects from gauge artefacts, which is particularly relevant for assessing the stability of perturbations in this theory. In the superhorizon limit, we obtain the leading-order behaviour of the relevant gauge-invariant variables and analyse the perturbations in several commonly used gauges. We find that the Newtonian gauge exhibits an apparent instability, characterised by the exponential growth of the metric perturbations. However, this growth is non-generic and gauge-dependent; in the other gauges analysed in this work, the perturbations remain well behaved within the perturbative regime. Physical observables can thus be consistently computed, and the apparent instability is identified as a gauge artefact rather than a pathology of the theory. Our analysis also demonstrates how the evolution behaviour of a gauge-invariant variable changes under the frame transformation and clarifies the relation between results obtained in the Jordan and Einstein frames.

    1 Introduction

    Inflation [15, 44, 45, 26, 31, 3] is a short period of accelerated expansion in the very early Universe that not only addresses the problems of the standard Big Bang framework, such as the horizon and flatness problems, but also seeds the primordial inhomogeneities through the quantum fluctuations of fields. After inflation, these fluctuations re-enter the Hubble horizon and leave measurable imprints in the cosmic microwave background (CMB) and the large-scale structure.

    Cosmological perturbation theory provides a mathematical framework to study the dynamics of inflation and to connect theory with observations [28, 35, 34]. It relies on the assumption that deviations from homogeneity and isotropy remain small, allowing for an expansion in perturbation variables around a homogeneous and isotropic background. It is therefore important to ensure that perturbations indeed remain within the perturbative regime. However, it should be stressed that the amplitude of perturbations is not a gauge-invariant statement; individual metric components may exhibit large or growing behaviour depending on the gauge choice, without implying a breakdown of the theory itself. Consequently, statements about the stability of solutions must ultimately rely on gauge-invariant quantities [12] or on physically meaningful observables such as the curvature perturbation ℛ\mathcal{R}.

    According to the latest observational constraints from the CMB experiments such as Planck [2], BICEP/Keck [1], and ACT [32, 16], one of the most successful inflationary scenarios is the Starobinsky model [45]. In this framework, the Einstein–Hilbert action is extended by a quadratic Ricci scalar R2R^{2} correction that introduces a scalar degree of freedom commonly referred to as the scalaron. This provides a purely geometrical origin of inflation and, in the Einstein frame, is equivalent to a single-field inflationary model with a plateau-like potential along which the field slowly rolls; see, e.g., Ref. [19] for a review. From a theoretical perspective, it is also well-motivated within the effective field theory (EFT) approach to gravity [22, 49], in which higher-curvature corrections naturally arise as higher-dimensional operators suppressed by a cutoff scale.

    Both the Starobinsky model and Einstein’s theory of general relativity are expected to break down at sufficiently high energies, as neither provides an ultraviolet (UV) completion of gravity. From an EFT perspective, the gravitational action can be extended by including higher powers of RR as well as other curvature invariants. To the second order in the curvature, a more natural extension of Einstein’s gravity theory would thus include R2R^{2}, Rμ​ν​Rμ​νR_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu}, and Rμ​ν​ρ​σ​Rμ​ν​ρ​σR_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}. The resulting framework, known as quadratic gravity, was first systematically studied by Stelle1 [46, 47], and it was shown that the inclusion of these additional quadratic terms renders the theory perturbatively renormalisable [24, 25, 10]. However, this improved UV behaviour comes at the cost of introducing additional propagating degrees of freedom. In addition to the massless graviton and the scalaron arising from the R2R^{2} term, a massive spin-2 mode and an additional scalar mode come into play [46, 47]; see also Refs. [20, 27, 42, 18].

    At the classical level, the presence of higher derivatives is associated with the Ostrogradsky instability [37], which manifests itself through an unbounded Hamiltonian; see also Ref. [53] for a review. At the quantum level, the massive spin-2 state, which comes with the wrong sign for the kinetic term, can be quantised with a negative norm to avoid this instability [38, 40, 42]. While this raises concerns about unitarity, it is important to note that quadratic gravity can still be consistently interpreted as an EFT valid below a cutoff scale, or as a sector of a more complete theory in which these issues may be resolved. Various approaches to dealing with the ghost degree of freedom have been explored in the literature [39, 23, 6, 7, 4, 13, 21, 43, 5, 29], with different phenomenological interpretations depending on the physical regime considered.

    In the context of inflationary cosmology, quadratic gravity has been previously studied at the level of linear perturbations [50, 17, 36, 20, 27, 41, 18, 14]. It has been found that the tensor sector remains well behaved, with the additional massive spin-2 mode not spoiling the standard evolution of gravitational waves. Vector modes, which are absent in standard single-field inflation, become dynamical in this theory; however, their amplitude decreases as inflation proceeds, and they therefore do not lead to observable consequences.

    In contrast, analyses of the scalar sector have led to differing interpretations. On the one hand, Ref. [20], where the analysis is done in the Einstein frame, concluded that the growth of certain perturbations observed in the Newtonian gauge is a gauge artefact rather than a physical instability. On the other hand, the more recent analysis of Ref. [18], where the analysis is performed in the Jordan frame, suggests that instabilities might persist even once the Newtonian gauge is avoided. It is important to note that these conclusions often rely on specific metric components or gauge-dependent variables, complicating their physical interpretation. Moreover, the different frame choices play a non-trivial role in comparing the two analyses.

    This situation motivates a careful reanalysis of the scalar sector in a fully gauge-independent manner. Such an approach allows us to isolate the physical degrees of freedom and to determine whether any instability is present at the level of observables. The aim of the present work is precisely to address this issue and to clarify the behaviour of scalar perturbations.

    2 Setup

    At quadratic order in the curvature, the gravitational action is not restricted to the R2R^{2} term that appears in Starobinsky inflation [45].2 In general, it may also include the scalars Rμ​ν​Rμ​νR_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu} and Rμ​ν​ρ​σ​Rμ​ν​ρ​σR_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}, leading to the most general local action built from curvature invariants up to second order.3 The corresponding action reads4

    S=MP22​∫d4​x​−g​[R+α​R2+β​Rμ​ν​Rμ​ν+γ​Rμ​ν​ρ​σ​Rμ​ν​ρ​σ],\displaystyle S=\frac{M_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}}{2}\int d^{4}x\,\sqrt{-g}\,\left[R+\alpha R^{2}+\beta R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu}+\gamma R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\right]\,, (2.1)

    where α\alpha, β\beta, and γ\gamma are constant coefficients of mass dimension −2-2, and MP≡1/8​π​GM_{\rm P}\equiv 1/\sqrt{8\pi G} is the reduced Planck mass.

    Using the fact that the Gauss–Bonnet term, which is given by

    𝒢=R2−4​Rμ​ν​Rμ​ν+Rμ​ν​ρ​σ​Rμ​ν​ρ​σ,\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=R^{2}-4R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu}+R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\,, (2.2)

    is topological in four dimensions [30, 33], the Riemann tensor-squared term, i.e., the γ\gamma term, can be eliminated in favour of R2R^{2} and Rμ​ν​Rμ​νR_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu}. The action can then be written as

    S=MP22​∫d4​x​−g​[R+(α−γ)​R2+(β+4​γ)​Rμ​ν​Rμ​ν].\displaystyle S=\frac{M_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}}{2}\int d^{4}x\,\sqrt{-g}\,\left[R+(\alpha-\gamma)R^{2}+(\beta+4\gamma)R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu}\right]\,. (2.3)

    Alternatively, using the identity relating the Weyl tensor-squared term to the other invariants, namely [51]

    Cμ​ν​ρ​σ​Cμ​ν​ρ​σ=𝒢−23​R2+2​Rμ​ν​Rμ​ν,\displaystyle C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}C^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}=\mathcal{G}-\frac{2}{3}R^{2}+2R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu}\,, (2.4)

    one may eliminate the Ricci tensor-squared term, arriving at the following action:

    S=MP22​∫d4​x​−g​[R+λ​R2−ω2​Cμ​ν​ρ​σ​Cμ​ν​ρ​σ],\displaystyle S=\frac{M_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}}{2}\int d^{4}x\,\sqrt{-g}\,\left[R+\lambda R^{2}-\frac{\omega}{2}C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}C^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\right]\,, (2.5)

    where the topological Gauss–Bonnet term has been ignored, and the coefficients λ\lambda and ω\omega are given by

    λ=α+13​(β+γ),ω=−(β+4​γ).\displaystyle\lambda=\alpha+\frac{1}{3}(\beta+\gamma)\,,\qquad\omega=-\left(\beta+4\gamma\right)\,. (2.6)

    The action (2.5) has recently been studied in Ref. [18] in the context of inflationary cosmology. The parameters λ\lambda and ω\omega are restricted to positive values in the current work so as to avoid tachyonic instabilities in the spectrum. Readers may refer to Ref. [18] for a more detailed discussion; see also e.g., Refs. [20, 27].

    It is well known that f​(R)f(R) gravity is equivalent to the scalar–tensor theory; see, for instance, Ref. [19] for a review. As such, the R2R^{2} term in the action (2.5), provided that its coefficient λ\lambda is non-zero, can be mapped onto a scalar degree of freedom via the introduction of an auxiliary field φ\varphi. The action then reads

    S=MP22​∫d4​x​−g​[R+2​λ​φ​R−λ​φ2−ω2​Cμ​ν​ρ​σ​Cμ​ν​ρ​σ].\displaystyle S=\frac{M_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}}{2}\int d^{4}x\,\sqrt{-g}\,\left[R+2\lambda\varphi R-\lambda\varphi^{2}-\frac{\omega}{2}C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}C^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\right]\,. (2.7)

    where we have used the index “E” to denote Einstein-frame quantities, and f​(φ)=1+2​λ​φf(\varphi)=1+2\lambda\varphi, the action (2.7) can be brought to the Einstein frame as follows:

    S=∫d4​x​−gE​[MP22​RE−12​gEμ​ν​∂μϕE​∂νϕE−VE−ω​MP24​Cμ​ν​ρ​σE​CEμ​ν​ρ​σ].\displaystyle S=\int d^{4}x\,\sqrt{-g^{\mathrm{E}}}\,\left[\frac{M_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}}{2}R^{\mathrm{E}}-\frac{1}{2}g_{\mathrm{E}}^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\phi^{\rm E}\partial_{\nu}\phi^{\rm E}-V^{\mathrm{E}}-\frac{\omega M^{2}_{\rm P}}{4}C^{\mathrm{E}}_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}C^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}_{\mathrm{E}}\right]\,.     3 Evolution of gauge-invariant variables

    From the three systems of perturbed equations of motion obtained in the previous section, namely Eqs. (2.26)–(2.28), Eqs. (2.30)–(2.32), and Eqs. (2.34)–(2.36), one can derive a closed evolution equation for each of the gauge-invariant variables. In this section, we present the resultant expressions for such evolution equations. We then analyse the dynamical behaviour of the gauge-invariant variables by solving the evolution equations in the dS limit and taking the superhorizon limit. The detailed derivation steps are relegated to Appendix A.

    It is convenient to perform the calculation in Fourier space, i.e.,

    X​(t,𝐱)=∫d3​k​X𝐤​(t)​ei​𝐤⋅𝐱,\displaystyle X(t,\mathbf{x})=\int d^{3}k\,X_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\,e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\,, (3.1)

    where XX represents the gauge-invariant variable, X={Ψ,Φ,ℛ,χ,𝒜,𝒜ψ}X=\{\Psi,\Phi,\mathcal{R},\chi,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A}_{\psi}\}. For simplicity, the wavenumber subscript “𝐤\mathbf{k}” will be dropped, X𝐤→XX_{\mathbf{k}}\rightarrow X.

    3.1 Evolution of Ψ\Psi and Φ\Phi

    From Eqs. (2.26)–(2.28), we obtain fourth-order evolution equations for the gauge-invariant variables Ψ\Psi and Φ\Phi as

    0\displaystyle 0 =Φ˙˙˙˙+H​α3​Φ˙˙˙+H2​α2​Φ¨+H3​α1​Φ˙+H4​α0​Φ,\displaystyle=\ddddot{\Phi}+H\alpha_{3}\dddot{\Phi}+H^{2}\alpha_{2}\ddot{\Phi}+H^{3}\alpha_{1}\dot{\Phi}+H^{4}\alpha_{0}\Phi\,, (3.2)
    0\displaystyle 0 =Ψ˙˙˙˙+H​β3​Ψ˙˙˙+H2​β2​Ψ¨+H3​β1​Ψ˙+H4​β0​Ψ.\displaystyle=\ddddot{\Psi}+H\beta_{3}\dddot{\Psi}+H^{2}\beta_{2}\ddot{\Psi}+H^{3}\beta_{1}\dot{\Psi}+H^{4}\beta_{0}\Psi\,. (3.3)

    In Appendix A, we provide more details of the intermediate steps leading to the evolution equations, as well as the coefficients αi\alpha_{i} and βi\beta_{i}. In the superhorizon limit where k≪a​Hk\ll aH, and to the zeroth order in the slow-roll parameters ϵi\epsilon_{i} (i=1,2,⋯i=1,2,\cdots), i.e., in the dS limit, Eqs. (3.2) and (3.3) become

    0\displaystyle 0 =Φ˙˙˙˙+(1H2​ω−1)​H2​Φ¨+Hω​Φ˙,\displaystyle=\ddddot{\Phi}+\left(\frac{1}{H^{2}\omega}-1\right)H^{2}\ddot{\Phi}+\frac{H}{\omega}\dot{\Phi}\,, (3.4)
    0\displaystyle 0 =Ψ˙˙˙˙+(1H2​ω−1)​H2​Ψ¨+Hω​Ψ˙.\displaystyle=\ddddot{\Psi}+\left(\frac{1}{H^{2}\omega}-1\right)H^{2}\ddot{\Psi}+\frac{H}{\omega}\dot{\Psi}\,. (3.5)

    Both Φ\Phi and Ψ\Psi obey identical evolution equations at this order of approximation. Using the ansatz Φ,Ψ∝ex​H​t\Phi,\Psi\propto e^{xHt} and solving the resulting algebraic equation for xx, we obtain

    Φ,Ψ\displaystyle\Phi,\Psi =C1Φ,Ψ+C2Φ,Ψ​e−H​t+K+Φ,Ψ​e12​(1+θ)​H​t+K−Φ,Ψ​e12​(1−θ)​H​t,\displaystyle=C^{\Phi,\Psi}_{1}+C^{\Phi,\Psi}_{2}e^{-Ht}+K^{\Phi,\Psi}_{+}e^{\frac{1}{2}(1+\theta)Ht}+K^{\Phi,\Psi}_{-}e^{\frac{1}{2}(1-\theta)Ht}\,, (3.6)

    where

    θ\displaystyle\theta ≡1−4H2​ω,\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{1-\frac{4}{H^{2}\omega}}\,, (3.7)

    and C1Φ,ΨC^{\Phi,\Psi}_{1}, C2Φ,ΨC^{\Phi,\Psi}_{2}, K+Φ,ΨK_{+}^{\Phi,\Psi}, and K−Φ,ΨK_{-}^{\Phi,\Psi} are constants of integration for Φ\Phi and Ψ\Psi.

    From Eq. (3.7), it is evident that ω=ωcrit≡4/H2\omega=\omega_{\mathrm{crit}}\equiv 4/H^{2} is a critical value to determine whether θ\theta is real or pure imaginary. In either case, however, the last two terms in the solution (3.6) always grow exponentially in time, causing the instability problem, as pointed out in Refs. [20, 27]; see also Ref. [18] for the analysis in the Jordan frame.

    3.2 Evolution of ℛ\mathcal{R}

    We now turn to the gauge-invariant variable ℛ\mathcal{R}, whose evolution equation takes the form

    0\displaystyle 0 =ℛ˙˙˙˙+H​γ3​ℛ˙˙˙+H2​γ2​ℛ¨+H3​γ1​ℛ˙+H4​γ0​ℛ.\displaystyle=\ddddot{\mathcal{R}}+H\gamma_{3}\dddot{\mathcal{R}}+H^{2}\gamma_{2}\ddot{\mathcal{R}}+H^{3}\gamma_{1}\dot{\mathcal{R}}+H^{4}\gamma_{0}\mathcal{R}\,. (3.8)

    The derivation steps and expressions for the coefficients γi\gamma_{i} up to 𝒪​(ϵi)\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{i}) are given in Appendix A. In the superhorizon limit, Eq. (3.8) reduces to

    0\displaystyle 0 =ℛ˙˙˙˙+6​H​ℛ˙˙˙+(11+1ω​H2)​H2​ℛ¨+(6+3H2​ω)​H3​ℛ˙,\displaystyle=\ddddot{\mathcal{R}}+6H\dddot{\mathcal{R}}+\left(11+\frac{1}{\omega H^{2}}\right)H^{2}\ddot{\mathcal{R}}+\left(6+\frac{3}{H^{2}\omega}\right)H^{3}\dot{\mathcal{R}}\,, (3.9)

    up to the zeroth order in the slow-roll parameters ϵi\epsilon_{i}. Similarly to the previous case of Φ\Phi and Ψ\Psi, with the ansatz ℛ∼ex​H​t\mathcal{R}\sim e^{xHt}, the leading-order solution is found as

    ℛ=C1ℛ+C2ℛ​e−3​H​t+K+ℛ​e−32​(1+θ3)​H​t+K−ℛ​e−32​(1−θ3)​H​t,\displaystyle\mathcal{R}=C_{1}^{\mathcal{R}}+C_{2}^{\mathcal{R}}e^{-3Ht}+K_{+}^{\mathcal{R}}e^{-\frac{3}{2}\left(1+\frac{\theta}{3}\right)Ht}+K_{-}^{\mathcal{R}}e^{-\frac{3}{2}\left(1-\frac{\theta}{3}\right)Ht}\,, (3.10)

    where θ\theta is defined in Eq. (3.7), and C1ℛC^{\mathcal{R}}_{1}, C2ℛC^{\mathcal{R}}_{2}, K+ℛK_{+}^{\mathcal{R}}, and K−ℛK_{-}^{\mathcal{R}} are integration constants. The first two terms correspond to the standard slow-roll solution, ℛSR∼C+D/a3\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{SR}}\sim C+D/a^{3}, consisting of a conserved mode and a decaying mode on superhorizon scales, while the additional two modes come from the Weyl-squared contribution.

    For ω<ωcrit=4/H2\omega<\omega_{\mathrm{crit}}=4/H^{2}, θ\theta is pure imaginary and the additional modes exhibit oscillating behaviour with exponentially decreasing amplitude. For ω>ωcrit=4/H2\omega>\omega_{\mathrm{crit}}=4/H^{2}, θ\theta is real, and the additional modes decay exponentially. In either case, the extra modes coming from the Weyl-squared term decay with time, implying that ℛ\mathcal{R} approaches a constant value on superhorizon scales, as in standard inflation. In other words, the gauge-invariant curvature perturbation ℛ\mathcal{R}, which is directly related to the CMB observables, freezes outside the Hubble horizon. This conclusion agrees with the result reported in Refs. [20, 27, 18].

    3.3 Evolution of χ\chi

    The gauge-invariant variable χ\chi satisfies

    0=χ˙˙˙˙+H​ν3​χ˙˙˙+H2​ν2​χ¨+H3​ν1​χ˙+H4​ν0​χ.\displaystyle 0=\ddddot{\chi}+H\nu_{3}\dddot{\chi}+H^{2}\nu_{2}\ddot{\chi}+H^{3}\nu_{1}\dot{\chi}+H^{4}\nu_{0}\chi\,. (3.11)

    The derivation steps and expressions for the coefficients νi\nu_{i} up to 𝒪​(ϵi)\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{i}) are given in Appendix A. In the superhorizon limit, together with the dS limit, the evolution equation for χ\chi reads

    0=χ˙˙˙˙+2​H​χ˙˙˙+(−3+1H2​ω)​H2​χ¨+3​Hω​χ˙.\displaystyle 0=\ddddot{\chi}+2H\dddot{\chi}+\left(-3+\frac{1}{H^{2}\omega}\right)H^{2}\ddot{\chi}+\frac{3H}{\omega}\dot{\chi}\,. (3.12)

    Hence, the superhorizon solution is given by

    χ=C1χ+C2χ​e−3​H​t+K+χ​e12​(1+θ)​H​t+K−χ​e12​(1−θ)​H​t,\displaystyle\chi=C^{\chi}_{1}+C_{2}^{\chi}e^{-3Ht}+K_{+}^{\chi}e^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\theta\right)Ht}+K_{-}^{\chi}e^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\theta\right)Ht}\,, (3.13)

    where θ\theta is defined in Eq. (3.7), and C1χC^{\chi}_{1}, C2χC^{\chi}_{2}, K+χK_{+}^{\chi}, and K−χK_{-}^{\chi} are integration constants.

    The dependence on ω\omega is the same as in the Ψ\Psi and Φ\Phi cases. The last two modes, introduced by the higher-derivative contributions associated with the Weyl-squared term, suffer from the exponential instability on superhorizon scales.

    3.4 Evolution of 𝒜\mathcal{A}

    The evolution equation for 𝒜\mathcal{A} is given by

    0=𝒜˙˙˙˙+H​μ3​𝒜˙˙˙+H2​μ2​𝒜¨+H3​μ1​𝒜˙+H4​μ0​𝒜.\displaystyle 0=\ddddot{\mathcal{A}}+H\mu_{3}\dddot{\mathcal{A}}+H^{2}\mu_{2}\ddot{\mathcal{A}}+H^{3}\mu_{1}\dot{\mathcal{A}}+H^{4}\mu_{0}\mathcal{A}\,. (3.14)

    The derivation steps and exact expressions for the coefficients μi\mu_{i} are given in Appendix A. Taking the same limit as before, namely the superhorizon limit and the dS limit, leads to the following equation:

    0=𝒜˙˙˙˙+12​H​𝒜˙˙˙+(53+1H2​ω)​H2​𝒜¨+(102+5H2​ω)​H3​𝒜˙+(72+6H2​ω)​H4​𝒜.\displaystyle 0=\ddddot{\mathcal{A}}+12H\dddot{\mathcal{A}}+\left(53+\frac{1}{H^{2}\omega}\right)H^{2}\ddot{\mathcal{A}}+\left(102+\frac{5}{H^{2}\omega}\right)H^{3}\dot{\mathcal{A}}+\left(72+\frac{6}{H^{2}\omega}\right)H^{4}\mathcal{A}\,. (3.15)

    The solution is given by

    𝒜=C1𝒜​e−2​H​t+C2𝒜​e−3​H​t+K+𝒜​e−72​(1+θ7)​H​t+K−𝒜​e−72​(1−θ7)​H​t,\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=C_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}e^{-2Ht}+C_{2}^{\mathcal{A}}e^{-3Ht}+K_{+}^{\mathcal{A}}e^{-\frac{7}{2}\left(1+\frac{\theta}{7}\right)Ht}+K_{-}^{\mathcal{A}}e^{-\frac{7}{2}\left(1-\frac{\theta}{7}\right)Ht}\,, (3.16)

    where θ\theta is defined in Eq. (3.7), and C1𝒜C^{\mathcal{A}}_{1}, C2𝒜C^{\mathcal{A}}_{2}, K+𝒜K_{+}^{\mathcal{A}}, and K−𝒜K_{-}^{\mathcal{A}} are integration constants.

    In the case of 𝒜\mathcal{A}, one can see that the dependence on ω\omega is the same as for ℛ\mathcal{R}. We conclude that all the modes of 𝒜\mathcal{A} decay at the leading order.

    3.5 Evolution of 𝒜ψ\mathcal{A}_{\psi}

    Finally, let us examine the evolution equation for 𝒜ψ\mathcal{A}_{\psi},

    0=𝒜˙˙˙˙ψ+H​ρ3​𝒜˙˙˙ψ+H2​ρ2​𝒜¨ψ+H3​ρ1​𝒜˙ψ+H4​ρ0​𝒜ψ.\displaystyle 0=\ddddot{\mathcal{A}}_{\psi}+H\rho_{3}\dddot{\mathcal{A}}_{\psi}+H^{2}\rho_{2}\ddot{\mathcal{A}}_{\psi}+H^{3}\rho_{1}\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{\psi}+H^{4}\rho_{0}\mathcal{A}_{\psi}\,. (3.17)

    The derivation steps and expressions for the coefficients ρi\rho_{i} up to 𝒪​(ϵi)\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{i}) are given in Appendix A. In the superhorizon limit, together with the dS limit, the evolution equation for 𝒜ψ\mathcal{A}_{\psi} becomes

    0=𝒜˙˙˙˙ψ+6​H​𝒜˙˙˙ψ+(11+1H2​ω)​H2​𝒜¨ψ+(6+3H2​ω)​H3​𝒜˙ψ.\displaystyle 0=\ddddot{\mathcal{A}}_{\psi}+6H\dddot{\mathcal{A}}_{\psi}+\left(11+\frac{1}{H^{2}\omega}\right)H^{2}\ddot{\mathcal{A}}_{\psi}+\left(6+\frac{3}{H^{2}\omega}\right)H^{3}\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{\psi}\,. (3.18)

    The solution is then given by

    𝒜ψ=C1𝒜ψ+C2𝒜ψ​e−3​H​t+K+𝒜ψ​e−32​(1+θ3)​H​t+K−𝒜ψ​e−32​(1−θ3)​H​t,\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\psi}=C_{1}^{\mathcal{A}_{\psi}}+C_{2}^{\mathcal{A}_{\psi}}e^{-3Ht}+K_{+}^{\mathcal{A}_{\psi}}e^{-\frac{3}{2}\left(1+\frac{\theta}{3}\right)Ht}+K_{-}^{\mathcal{A}_{\psi}}e^{-\frac{3}{2}\left(1-\frac{\theta}{3}\right)Ht}\,, (3.19)

    where θ\theta is defined in Eq. (3.7), and C1𝒜ψC^{\mathcal{A}_{\psi}}_{1}, C2𝒜ψC^{\mathcal{A}_{\psi}}_{2}, K+𝒜ψK_{+}^{\mathcal{A}_{\psi}}, and K−𝒜ψK_{-}^{\mathcal{A}_{\psi}} are integration constants.

    We note that the 𝒜ψ\mathcal{A}_{\psi} solution (3.19) takes the same form as the ℛ\mathcal{R} solution (3.10). In other words, we have three decaying modes and one constant mode. This outcome, which is found in a gauge-independent way, agrees with the analysis of Ref. [20]. However, it differs from the result of Ref. [18], where a linearly growing solution is reported instead of the constant mode.

    In Ref. [18], the linearly growing mode is found in the flat gauge to take the form

    𝒜ψDF≈c4​(1+t18​λ​H),\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\psi}^{\rm DF}\approx c_{4}\left(1+\frac{t}{18\lambda H}\right)\,, (3.20)

    where λ\lambda, which corresponds to β\beta in the notation of Ref. [18], is given in Eq. (2.6), c4c_{4} is the integration constant, and we have put the superscript “DF” to indicate that it is the solution reported in Ref. [18]. It is important to note that the analysis of Ref. [18] is performed in the Jordan frame, where the action takes the form of Eq. (2.5). Therefore, HH, tt, and 𝒜ψDF\mathcal{A}_{\psi}^{\rm DF} are actually the Jordan-frame quantities. Furthermore, the second term in Eq. (3.20) is of first order in the slow-roll parameters; in the Jordan frame, the first Hubble slow-roll parameter is approximately given by ϵ1J≈1/(36​λ​H2)\epsilon_{1}^{\rm J}\approx 1/(36\lambda H^{2}). To the zeroth order in the slow-roll parameters, the solution (3.20) would thus correspond to a constant mode.

    In our case, where the analysis is performed in the Einstein frame, the constant solution is the outcome of the usage of the leading-order contributions in the slow-roll parameters. As shown in Eq. (A.50), the leading order for ρ0\rho_{0} is already 𝒪​(ϵ2)\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{2}). If we take into account the contributions from terms of first order in the slow-roll parameters, the solution that corresponds to the constant solution 𝒜ψ=C1𝒜ψ\mathcal{A}_{\psi}=C_{1}^{\mathcal{A}_{\psi}} becomes

    𝒜ψ=C1𝒜ψ​eϵ2​H​t≈C1𝒜ψ​(1+ϵ2​H​t).\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\psi}=C_{1}^{\mathcal{A}_{\psi}}e^{\epsilon_{2}Ht}\approx C_{1}^{\mathcal{A}_{\psi}}\left(1+\epsilon_{2}Ht\right)\,. (3.21)

    This next-to-leading order matches the linear-growth behaviour found in Ref. [18]. We stress again that 𝒜ψ\mathcal{A}_{\psi}, HH, ϵ2\epsilon_{2}, and tt in Eq. (3.21) are the Einstein-frame quantities. We can also show via Weyl rescaling that the solutions, Eq. (3.20) and Eq. (3.21), are, in fact, equivalent. The detailed derivation is presented in Appendix B.

    It is also worth mentioning that if we use the relation,

    𝒜ψ=𝒜−dd​t​(ℛH),\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\psi}=\mathcal{A}-\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}}{H}\right)\,, (3.22)

    which comes from the definitions of 𝒜ψ\mathcal{A}_{\psi} and 𝒜\mathcal{A}, substituting the solutions (3.10) and (3.16) would lead to the incorrect result of 𝒜ψ∝e−3​H​t/2\mathcal{A}_{\psi}\propto e^{-3Ht/2}. This is because the approximated solutions do not carry the information about the constraints between the variables. This is where the gauge-independent approach proves useful, as no variables are constructed from other variables; each evolution equation for the gauge-invariant variables is worked out from the system of equations consisting of gauge-invariant variables.

    4 Analysis of gauges

    As the analysis performed in Sec. 3 is carried out in terms of the gauge-invariant variables, the results are valid in any gauge. However, the analysis of the metric perturbations differs depending on the gauge choice. In this section, we analyse how the metric variables evolve in several commonly used gauges.

    Let us first summarise the leading-order behaviour of the gauge-invariant variables found in the previous section:

    Φ,Ψ,χ∝e12​(1+θ)​H​t,ℛ≃const.,𝒜∝e−2​H​t,𝒜ψ≃const.\displaystyle\Phi,\Psi,\chi\propto e^{\frac{1}{2}(1+\theta)Ht}\,,\quad\mathcal{R}\simeq\text{const.}\,,\quad\mathcal{A}\propto e^{-2Ht}\,,\quad\mathcal{A}_{\psi}\simeq\text{const.} (4.1)

    Strictly speaking, the leading-order behaviour of Φ\Phi, Ψ\Psi, and χ\chi depends on the value of ω\omega. If ω<4/H2\omega<4/H^{2}, we would have Φ,Ψ,χ∝eH​t/2\Phi,\Psi,\chi\propto e^{Ht/2}, while if ω>4/H2\omega>4/H^{2}, the growth rate ranges from eH​t/2e^{Ht/2} to eH​te^{Ht}. However, as the exact dependence is irrelevant for the qualitative conclusions of our analysis, we shall consider the value of ω\omega that leads to the weakest growth rate of the dominant mode, i.e., eH​t/2e^{Ht/2}, throughout this section.

    Newtonian gauge

    The Newtonian gauge is defined by the conditions E=0E=0 and B=0B=0. The remaining metric variables AA and ψ\psi reduce to

    ψN=Ψ∝e12​H​t,AN=Φ∝e12​H​t.\displaystyle\psi_{\mathrm{N}}=\Psi\propto e^{\frac{1}{2}Ht}\,,\quad A_{\mathrm{N}}=\Phi\propto e^{\frac{1}{2}Ht}\,. (4.2)

    It is also possible to read the scalar-field perturbation; it behaves as

    δ​ϕNϕ¯˙=χ∝e12​H​t.\displaystyle\frac{\delta\phi_{\mathrm{N}}}{\dot{\bar{\phi}}}=\chi\propto e^{\frac{1}{2}Ht}\,. (4.3)

    As such, in the Newtonian gauge, all the perturbation variables grow exponentially with time. As a consequence, the fundamental assumption of perturbation theory that perturbations remain small compared to the background quantities is violated. In other words, the linear perturbation theory breaks down in this gauge. This implies that the Newtonian gauge is not suitable for analysing the perturbative dynamics in this framework.

    Synchronous gauge

    The synchronous gauge is defined by the conditions A=0A=0 and B=0B=0. The metric variables behave as

    ψS=−H​∫𝒜ψ​𝑑t∝t,ES=∫1a2​(∫Φ​𝑑t′)​𝑑t∝e−32​H​t,\displaystyle\psi_{\mathrm{S}}=-H\int\mathcal{A}_{\psi}\,dt\propto t\,,\quad E_{\mathrm{S}}=\int\frac{1}{a^{2}}\left(\int\Phi\,dt^{\prime}\right)dt\propto e^{-\frac{3}{2}Ht}\,, (4.4)

    and the scalar-field perturbation behaves as

    δ​ϕSϕ¯˙=−∫𝒜​𝑑t∝e−2​H​t.\displaystyle\frac{\delta\phi_{\mathrm{S}}}{\dot{\bar{\phi}}}=-\int\mathcal{A}\,dt\propto e^{-2Ht}\,. (4.5)

    The metric variable ψS\psi_{\mathrm{S}} exhibits a linear growth, and the other quantities in this gauge decay.

    Flat slicing

    The flat slicing is fixed by the condition ψ=0\psi=0. For the threading, i.e., the spatial coordinate transformation, one may choose either B=0B=0 or E=0E=0. In this gauge, the perturbations evolve as

    δ​ϕFϕ¯˙=−ℛH≃const.,AF=𝒜ψ≃const.,\displaystyle\frac{\delta\phi_{\mathrm{F}}}{\dot{\bar{\phi}}}=-\frac{\mathcal{R}}{H}\simeq\text{const.}\,,\quad A_{\mathrm{F}}=\mathcal{A}_{\psi}\simeq\text{const.}\,, (4.6)

    and depending on the threading, we have either

    EF=∫Ψa2​H​𝑑t∝e−32​H​t,\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{F}}=\int\frac{\Psi}{a^{2}H}\,dt\propto e^{-\frac{3}{2}Ht}\,, (4.7)

    for B=0B=0, or

    BF=Ψa​H∝e−12​H​t,\displaystyle B_{\mathrm{F}}=\frac{\Psi}{aH}\propto e^{-\frac{1}{2}Ht}\,, (4.8)

    for E=0E=0. Note that AFA_{\mathrm{F}} corresponds to the gauge-invariant variable 𝒜ψ\mathcal{A}_{\psi} shown in Eq. (3.22). All the perturbations in the flat gauge either decay or remain constant in time. Therefore, the linear perturbation theory remains valid.

    Comoving slicing

    The comoving slicing is fixed by the condition δ​ϕ=0\delta\phi=0. For the threading, the literature typically adopts either B=0B=0 or E=0E=0. In this gauge, the metric perturbations are

    ψC=ℛ≃const.,AC=𝒜∝e−2​H​t,\displaystyle\psi_{\mathrm{C}}=\mathcal{R}\simeq\text{const.}\,,\quad A_{\mathrm{C}}=\mathcal{A}\propto e^{-2Ht}\,, (4.9)

    and depending on the choice for the threading, one may have

    EC=∫χa2​𝑑t∝e−32​H​t,\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{C}}=\int\frac{\chi}{a^{2}}\,dt\propto e^{-\frac{3}{2}Ht}\,, (4.10)

    for B=0B=0, or

    BC=χa∝e−12​H​t,\displaystyle B_{\mathrm{C}}=\frac{\chi}{a}\propto e^{-\frac{1}{2}Ht}\,, (4.11)

    for E=0E=0. The leading-order contributions are either constant or decaying with time. Consequently, the linear perturbation theory remains valid; the comoving slicing exhibits bounded metric perturbations.

    In Ref. [18], working in the Jordan frame, the authors analysed the unitary gauge defined by δ​R=0\delta R=0 and E=0E=0, which corresponds to δ​ϕ=0\delta\phi=0 and E=0E=0 in the Einstein frame. In particular, they showed that the gauge-invariant variable,

    ℬ≡a​(B+a​E˙)+ψ/H,\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\equiv a(B+a\dot{E})+\psi/H\,, (4.12)

    grows as ℬ∝eH​t/2\mathcal{B}\propto e^{Ht/2}. On this basis, it was argued that the growth of the metric component g0​i=a​∂iBg_{0i}=a\partial_{i}B signals an instability. In our analysis of the comoving slice, as BC∝e−H​t/2B_{\mathrm{C}}\propto e^{-Ht/2}, we see that g0​i=a​∂iBC∝eH​t/2g_{0i}=a\partial_{i}B_{\mathrm{C}}\propto e^{Ht/2} also features such a growth. Notably, as we have demonstrated earlier, the same behaviour arises in the flat gauge as well. However, we propose a different physical interpretation of this behaviour.

    First, the growth of g0​ig_{0i} reflects a gauge effect rather than a physical instability. By performing an appropriate gauge transformation, e.g., imposing B=0B=0, the growing behaviour in g0​ig_{0i} is removed, while all the other scalar perturbations remain bounded, resulting in an isotropic solution. In particular, the curvature perturbation ℛ\mathcal{R} remains constant on superhorizon scales. This indicates that the growth does not correspond to a dynamical degree of freedom.

    Second, a growing g0​ig_{0i} component may break the causality condition, thus making the coordinate system, i.e., the gauge choice, pathological [48]. However, by examining whether the congruence defined by the worldlines of d​xi=0dx^{i}=0 remains timelike throughout the inflationary trajectory, one may show that the growing g0​ig_{0i} does not lead to the violation of causality in the present case.6 Let us consider the ADM decomposition [8, 9, 52],

    d​s2=−α2​d​t2+γi​j​(d​xi+βi​d​t)​(d​xj+βj​d​t),\displaystyle ds^{2}=-\alpha^{2}dt^{2}+\gamma_{ij}\left(dx^{i}+\beta^{i}dt\right)\left(dx^{j}+\beta^{j}dt\right)\,, (4.13)

    where α\alpha is the lapse function, βi\beta^{i} is the shift vector, and γi​j\gamma_{ij} is the spatial metric. To linear order, we identify

    αC≃1+AC,βi​C=a​∂iBC.\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathrm{C}}\simeq 1+A_{\mathrm{C}}\,,\quad\beta_{i\mathrm{C}}=a\partial_{i}B_{\mathrm{C}}\,. (4.14)

    For the threading condition E=0E=0, the spatial metric at linear order is given by

    γCi​j≃a−2​(1−2​ψC)​δi​j,\displaystyle\gamma^{ij}_{\mathrm{C}}\simeq a^{-2}(1-2\psi_{\mathrm{C}})\delta^{ij}\,, (4.15)

    from which it follows that

    βCi≃a−1​δi​j​∂jBC.\displaystyle\beta^{i}_{\mathrm{C}}\simeq a^{-1}\delta^{ij}\partial_{j}B_{\mathrm{C}}\,. (4.16)

    We see from Eq. (4.13) that

    g00​C=−αC2+βi​C​βCi≃−(1+AC)2+∂iBC​∂iBC<0.\displaystyle g_{00\mathrm{C}}=-\alpha^{2}_{\mathrm{C}}+\beta_{i\mathrm{C}}\beta^{i}_{\mathrm{C}}\simeq-(1+A_{\mathrm{C}})^{2}+\partial_{i}B_{\mathrm{C}}\partial^{i}B_{\mathrm{C}}<0\,. (4.17)

    Therefore, the comoving slice, despite exhibiting a growing g0​ig_{0i}, preserves the timelike character of the congruence of worldlines d​xi=0dx^{i}=0, and hence, it does not break the causal structure and defines a regular coordinate system. Following the same reasoning, one may also arrive at the same conclusion for the flat gauge.

    In summary, the apparent exponential instability of the Newtonian gauge is absent in all the other gauges considered; the synchronous, flat, and comoving gauges are well-behaved coordinate systems. This observation confirms that the instability seen in the Newtonian gauge is a gauge artefact rather than a physical effect.

    5 Conclusion

    In this work, we have investigated the stability of scalar perturbations during inflation in quadratic gravity with a Weyl-squared term. Working in the Einstein frame, we have adopted a gauge-independent approach based on gauge-invariant variables, which allows us to distinguish genuine physical effects from gauge artefacts.

    We have derived the equations of motion for the scalar perturbations and expressed them explicitly in terms of the gauge-invariant variables. The resulting evolution equations have then been solved in the superhorizon limit and in the de Sitter limit. This analysis has allowed us to determine the leading-order time dependence of the relevant gauge-invariant variables. We have shown that while some of these variables exhibit exponentially growing behaviour, the physically relevant observables remain well behaved. In particular, the curvature perturbation ℛ\mathcal{R} approaches a constant on superhorizon scales, consistent with the behaviour expected in standard single-field inflationary scenarios.

    To better understand the implications for the scalar perturbations, we have subsequently analysed several commonly used gauge choices. In the Newtonian gauge, we found that the scalar perturbations grow exponentially with time. Consequently, they may eventually become comparable to the background quantities, apparently invalidating the perturbative expansion in this gauge. On the other hand, in the other gauges considered, namely the flat, comoving, and synchronous gauges, no such exponential instability has been observed. We have thus identified the exponential instability observed in the Newtonian gauge as a gauge artefact rather than a physical instability.

    Our results are consistent with those of Ref. [20]. The Weyl-squared term in quadratic gravity does not make the inflationary background unviable, provided that a well-defined gauge is chosen. Additionally, our work extends the analysis of Ref. [20] by working systematically with multiple sets of gauge-invariant variables, which clarifies the role of the constraints between them.

    We have also shown that our Einstein-frame analysis, once the frame difference is properly taken into account, agrees with the Jordan-frame analysis performed in Ref. [18], which reopened the question of the phenomenological viability of quadratic gravity. However, our physical interpretation of the results differs from that of Ref. [18]. In the comoving gauge, for instance, instead of identifying the growth of the metric component g0​ig_{0i} as a signal of the breakdown of cosmological perturbation theory, we have emphasised that the apparent instability does not necessarily propagate to the physical observables. This conclusion rests on the facts that the curvature perturbation ℛ\mathcal{R}, which is directly related to CMB observables, approaches a constant on superhorizon scales, and that the causal structure remains unbroken. Furthermore, as we have demonstrated, the scalar perturbations remain bounded in several regular, well-defined gauges.

    We stress that our analysis is restricted to linear perturbations around the flat FLRW background in the superhorizon limit with the de Sitter approximation. A number of important questions therefore remain open. First, it would be meaningful to extend the present analysis beyond the leading-order slow-roll approximation in order to assess how the growing modes behave in a realistic inflationary background where the Hubble parameter varies in time. Second, a detailed computation of the cosmological observables, such as the power spectrum, the spectral index, and the tensor-to-scalar ratio, in the presence of the Weyl-squared term would allow for a direct comparison with current CMB observational data. Finally, our analysis does not address the fate of the massive spin-2 ghost mode. We leave these important questions for future work.

    Appendix B Conformal transformation

    The conformal transformation, also known as the Weyl rescaling, allows one to go from one frame to another. For instance, through the Weyl rescaling, we can change the action (2.7), which we call the Jordan-frame action, to the Einstein-frame action (2.9). In this section, we first investigate relationships between Jordan-frame quantities and Einstein-frame quantities. We then discuss the behaviour of the gauge-invariant variable 𝒜ψ\mathcal{A}_{\psi} in both frames. In the following discussion, we shall use the superscript J (E) to denote the Jordan (Einstein) frame.

    Let us recall the conformal transformation we considered in Sec. 2,

    gμ​νJ→gμ​νE=f​(φ)​gμ​νJ,\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{J}}\to g_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{E}}=f(\varphi)g_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{J}}\,, (B.1)

    where φ\varphi is the auxiliary field which is identified with the Ricci scalar in the Jordan frame, namely φ=RJ\varphi=R^{\mathrm{J}}. Comparing the line element in the Jordan frame to that in the Einstein frame at the background level, one may see that

    d​tE=f​d​tJ,aE=f​aJ,\displaystyle dt^{\mathrm{E}}=\sqrt{f}dt^{\mathrm{J}}\,,\quad a^{\mathrm{E}}=\sqrt{f}a^{\mathrm{J}}\,, (B.2)

    HE=1f​HJ+f′​φ˙2​f3/2,\displaystyle H^{\mathrm{E}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{f}}H^{\mathrm{J}}+\frac{f^{\prime}\dot{\varphi}}{2f^{3/2}}\,, (B.3)

    where φ˙≡d​φ/d​tJ\dot{\varphi}\equiv d\varphi/dt^{\mathrm{J}}.

    AE=AJ+f′​δ​φ2​f,ψE=ψJ+f′​δ​φ2​f,BE=BJ,EE=EJ,\displaystyle A^{\mathrm{E}}=A^{\mathrm{J}}+\frac{f^{\prime}\delta\varphi}{2f}\,,\quad\psi^{\mathrm{E}}=\psi^{\mathrm{J}}+\frac{f^{\prime}\delta\varphi}{2f}\,,\quad B^{\mathrm{E}}=B^{\mathrm{J}}\,,\quad E^{\mathrm{E}}=E^{\mathrm{J}}\,, (B.4)
