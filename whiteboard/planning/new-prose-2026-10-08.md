## Layout patterns: The main content comes first

- Before choosing components, decide what the page is for and which content is primary. Everything else is arranged so it does not push the primary content down.
- A page either sends the reader on or is where they work. A page that sends the reader on, such as a list of sections or reports, shows each destination as a card that links, with only what helps the reader choose. A page where the reader reads, compares or acts on records uses tables and filters.
- Leave detailed figures for the page they belong to. A card shows a name and, at most, a count or a mark that something needs attention; the figures are on the page it opens.
- The page heading is followed by the main content. At most one short line comes between them. When the data was last updated, a refresh control and an explanation of the figures are secondary: put them in one short line after the main content's heading, below the main content, or in an accordion.
- Mark what needs attention where it is. A status tag on the card or row, not an alert that lists the items, which the reader then has to find.
- Only the page's main action is a primary button. Refresh, export and other secondary actions are text buttons.
- Show readers what they can use, without labelling their access. A page shows each reader only what their permissions allow. It does not label parts of the page with who is allowed to see them.
- A page zone is for supporting content. A tinted band at the top is not a place for the main content; on a dense page it pushes the main content down.

## Cards: Cards that link

- A card that is one link, for a page whose job is to send the reader somewhere else: the whole card opens the page its heading names.
- The heading's link covers the whole card, so the card is one target and one tab stop. Hover and keyboard focus mark the whole card.
- The only link in the card. A second link or a button inside it cannot be reached by pointer, because the heading's link lies over it.
- A card that needs attention says so on the card itself. See Tags.

## Header: Two levels of navigation

- An area with its own sections, each with several views, such as a report with parts and views. The sections are the secondary navigation under the header; the views of the current section are a second row inside the page.
- The first level, directly under the header: the sections of the area.
- Names what the page is about, such as the subject, so the reader knows where they are without breadcrumbs. The demo uses a smaller heading because it sits inside this page.
- Choices that apply to every view, such as the subject, come before the views.
- The second level: the views of the current section, in the content column, with the same current marking as the first level. Each view is its own page.
- Two levels at most. A third level is a sign the content needs restructuring.
- Each row keeps the same links on every page it appears on. Only the current link changes.
- Choices that apply to one view go inside that view, below the second row.
- When the views share one address and swap in place, use tabs for the second level instead.

## Layout patterns: Page zones (rule added later on 2026-10-08)

- Dense pages do not use page zones. On an admin tool or a data view, where the reader needs to find and act on content quickly, a tinted band pushes the main content too far down the page. Separate content with spacing and headings instead.

## Compact filter bar and combining components (later on 2026-10-08)

- Tables, Filter bar, "Compact: one row" class key: A filter bar whose filters are the compact .ds-filter controls lays them out on one row, with less padding. Use it where a full filter bar would push the content down, such as beside navigation. Apply is a secondary .ds-btn, sized to the row.
- Header, Two levels of navigation, .ds-subnav: The first row, directly under the header. Here it holds two groups: a <nav> of the area's sections, then a compact filter bar for choices that apply to every view, such as the subject. The groups sit twice as far apart as the items inside them.
- Spacing, proximity rule (added): The same holds across a row: groups of controls side by side sit at least twice as far apart as the controls inside each group.
- Using, Combining components: Most areas of a page can be built by putting existing components together. Look for a combination before inventing a component.
- Examples in these docs. The search form with the results' settings under it; a table with its toolbar of filters and bulk actions; a sidebar of accordions; a card that links, with a tag; secondary navigation with a compact filter bar.
- Each component works inside another. Use a component's documented markup unchanged inside the other.
- A new class is the last step. If a combination needs CSS, it is a rule on the outer component about what it can hold, written in the design system. Ask before adding a component.
