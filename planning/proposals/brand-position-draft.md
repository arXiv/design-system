# DRAFT — arXiv's competitive position

**Status: first draft for Shamsi to edit.** Written 2026-08-12 to answer a specific
brief: state where arXiv sits relative to the rest of research publishing, and argue it,
rather than laying out a landscape and leaving the reader to infer the conclusion.

Intended home: a new section in [BRAND.md](../../docs/BRAND.md), between *Brand statement*
and *Voice*. Kept separate until it has been edited, because BRAND.md is canonical and
agents read it as settled fact.

**Read the honesty note at the bottom before circulating this.** Roughly half of it is
measured and half is asserted, and the two are easy to tell apart only if someone points
at them.

## Where arXiv sits

Research publishing has one house style, and everybody follows it. Of the eighteen
platforms in [the brand-color audit](../../verification/audits/audit-brand-color.md) —
publishers, preprint servers, repositories, discovery tools — **twelve use a dark blue as
their primary brand color.** Not merely blue: the same blue. Eight of the twelve sit
between hue 200 and 210 on the color wheel, a band so narrow that Springer Nature, IEEE,
and Taylor & Francis are separated more by lightness than by color.

That uniformity is a choice, and it says something. Dark blue is the color of institutions
that want to be trusted because they are old. It signals authority through gravity: we
have been here a long time, we are serious, we are careful. Every one of these
organizations is competing for the same attribute, in the same register, with the same
tool.

**arXiv took the same hue and rejected the darkness.** Open Blue sits at hue 207 — dead
center of that crowded band, closer to the establishment's blue than most of them are to
each other. But at 82% lightness it is **thirty points lighter than the lightest platform
in the field**, and seventy points lighter than the darkest.

| | Hue | Lightness |
|---|---|---|
| The field (12 platforms) | 200–245 | 12%–52% |
| Taylor & Francis, the darkest | 205 | 12% |
| Zenodo, the lightest | 216 | 52% |
| **arXiv Open Blue** | **207** | **82%** |

This is the position, and it is deliberate: **recognizably part of the field, unmistakably
not one of them.** The hue earns arXiv a place in the ecosystem — nobody mistakes it for a
startup or a blog. The lightness is the argument. arXiv is not asking to be trusted because
it is imposing. It is asking to be trusted because it is open, and it looks it.

The same move repeats in the second color. Most of the field pairs its navy with black or
a cool grey. arXiv pairs Open Blue with Repository Brown — a near-black that is warm rather
than neutral, at 10% saturation on the orange side of the wheel. Warmth where the field is
cold, lightness where the field is heavy. That is the whole visual strategy in two colors.

## What this buys, and what it costs

**What it buys.** A researcher who has thirty publisher tabs open can find the arXiv one
without reading it. Distinctiveness in a crowded field is worth more than conformity, and
it is nearly free — the hue already does the work of belonging.

**What it costs, honestly.** Light blue reads as less imposing than navy, and *imposing* is
not always the wrong thing to be. Anyone arguing that arXiv should look more like a
publisher is not being foolish; they are trading distinctiveness for borrowed gravity. The
counterargument is that arXiv does not need borrowed gravity. It is thirty-five years old,
it is where the physics is, and its authority comes from what it holds rather than from
how solemnly it presents itself. A brand that has earned its standing can afford to look
approachable. One that has not, cannot.

**Where this constrains us.** The position only holds if the lightness is real rather than
cosmetic. A light-looking site that is slow, that gates content, that counts views and
ranks papers, would read as a marketing exercise — and the visual distinctiveness would
make the hypocrisy more visible, not less. The palette commits arXiv to behaving the way it
looks. That is why the no-metrics rule and the no-dark-patterns rule sit in the design
policies as hard constraints rather than preferences.

## The one open question this raises

ORCID's lime (`#a6ce39`) is the only lime in the eighteen, and it is close to arXiv's
Access Lime (`#c4d82e`). Access Lime is currently staff-only, so the two never appear in
the same context and no conflict exists today. If arXiv ever brings lime onto public
pages, that similarity becomes a question worth asking rather than an accident to discover
later.

## Honesty note — what is measured and what is asserted

Keeping these apart matters, because the measured parts will survive a skeptical reading
and the asserted parts will not.

**Measured.** Everything about color. The eighteen platforms, their hex values, the hue and
lightness figures, and the position of arXiv's colors within that field all come from
Shamsi's audit of 2026-07-01 plus arithmetic on those values. Anyone can check it.

**Asserted, and currently unevidenced.** Three claims that BRAND.md already makes and that
this draft leans on:

1. *"Publisher sites bury core tasks behind many clicks; arXiv surfaces them."* Plausible
   and probably true, but nobody has counted. A short audit — clicks to the PDF, the DOI,
   the citation, and the previous version, across six platforms — would turn this from a
   belief into a fact, and it is a couple of hours of work.
2. *"Best-in-class for speed."* No benchmark exists in the repo. Load-time comparisons
   against the same six would be straightforward.
3. *What the dark blue signals.* That dark blue reads as conservative authority is a
   widely-held design view and consistent with who uses it, but it is interpretation, not
   measurement. It is stated here as a reading rather than a finding.

**Not claimed at all**, because there is no evidence for it here: anything about market
share, traffic, cost, or how these platforms compare as products beyond their color. If
leadership needs those, they are a different piece of research.

## Suggested next step

If the argument holds up, the cheapest thing that would strengthen it most is the
click-count audit in point 1. It converts the strongest functional claim from an assertion
into a table, and a table of click counts is the kind of evidence that survives a room
full of skeptics better than any adjective does.
