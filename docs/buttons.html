Buttons

Every arXiv button — public and internal — shares one mechanical spec: 6px radius, 10px 20px padding, subtle rest shadow, 1px press drop, 0.12s transitions. What differs is the color, and the color is doing a job: it tells the person which context they are in. Detailed per-context references: public · internal.

One system, two contexts

The primary-action color is set by the context, not by preference (DESIGN-POLICIES, Internal vs. Public pages).

Public — Open Blue

Everything a reader or author sees on arxiv.org: listing, abstract, HTML paper pages. Primary is Open Blue #a5d6fe with Repository Brown text; secondary is white with a warm-grey gradient border.

Internal — Access Lime

Staff-facing tools: the moderation queue, arXiv Check, the Admin Console. Primary is Access Lime #c4d82e; secondary is a lime tint with a lime border.

Never mix the two accent colors

A lime primary on a public page, or an Open Blue primary in a staff tool, is a policy violation. If the context is ambiguous, settle it first and record the answer in a code comment.

Construction
Both filled variants share the V3 build: a 1.5px gradient border (lighter top → darker bottom) from dual padding-box/border-box backgrounds, plus an inner vignette. Hover brightens; press drops 1px and deepens the vignette.
Quiet tier
The text button borrows the link’s quietness, not its identity: Link Blue, never underlined, and hover is a background wash — the same feedback the other buttons give. Underlines stay with links, so a reader can still tell “this navigates” from “this acts here.”
No outline tier
The secondary is already white fill plus border, and on a white canvas a transparent fill is the same pixels as a white one. Each context reaches its quiet tier by removing whatever its secondary uses to hold the page: the public secondary drops its border, the internal one drops its lime fill and keeps a border (.btn-tertiary).
Icons
Any .ds-btn accepts an inline SVG. The stylesheet sizes it in em, blocks the baseline gap, and stops it shrinking beside a long label. Set stroke="currentColor" and nothing else.
Disabled
One flat Card Grey fill for every variant. An action that is merely not available yet takes aria-disabled instead — see Buttons in forms.

On tinted and dark surfaces

The primary holds its own on every field — its gradient border and vignette keep the edge legible cool-on-cool, cool-on-warm and on a committed color field alike. The secondary is the one that adapts.

Cool tint — reader footer band (.on-tint)

Warm tint — Card Grey section fill (secondary unchanged)

Committed color field — outreach masthead (.on-dark)

Relevant classes
.on-tint
On a cool tint, swaps the secondary’s white fill for Card Grey (same luminance, warm hue) so it reads as secondary by material rather than by contrast. On a warm tint the default white secondary already sits comfortably.
.on-dark
For a field dark enough to host white text: an outreach masthead, a photo band. A white fill would read as a paper rectangle stuck to the surface, louder than the primary beside it; .on-dark drops the fill so the field shows through and carries the boundary on a white border. See outreach sites for where such fields are allowed.
Not this
A default white secondary on a cool tint pops too hard and reads as primary — that is the case .on-tint exists for. Do not put .on-tint on a warm tint: its Card Grey fill melts into the surface. And .on-dark is only correct on a dark field; on a light tint use .on-tint.

Buttons in forms

A form ends in a row of buttons, and the only question that row asks is what tier the second button takes. Secondary is for a real alternative path — somewhere the person might genuinely be going. Text is for the null action, which is simply not proceeding. Every example is rendered with the public classes, and the same reasoning picks the internal tiers; field construction and validation live in Forms & validation.

One action

Relevant classes
The other actionTierWhy
Not proceeding — Cancel, Not nowTextThere is no second destination, so nothing is being ranked. A border would make the absence compete with the action.
A real alternative path — Go back, Save draftSecondaryBoth buttons lead somewhere. The row is ranking two destinations, not offering a way out of one. Go back is the previous step with the person's work still in it: a destination, not an absence.
A misclick is expensive — backing out discards work, or proceeding is destructiveSecondaryA text button beside a primary is easy to hit by accident, and neither discarded work nor a deleted file comes back. The weight buys a moment of pause.
Three or more actionsA secondary must appearText plus primary carries two ranks and no more. A borderless control between two bordered ones reads as a gap rather than a rank, so the row runs secondary, secondary, primary, and separates the backwards step by position instead of by weight.
No alternative and no null actionPrimary aloneThe person submits or leaves the page, which the browser already handles. Adding a Cancel invents a second choice and asks every reader to rule it out.
Cheap vs. costly
Weight is the tool for cheap mistakes. When the mistake is expensive or cannot be undone, the answer is a confirmation step, not a heavier button — arXiv already does this during submission, where deleting files raises a dialog naming how many files will be deleted. Weighting a button is not a substitute for confirming a destructive action.

A destructive action

The red is a warning, not a safeguard

Reserve .ds-btn-destructive for hard-delete and irreversible actions, and always pair it with a confirmation step. The color tells a person what the button costs; it does not stop them pressing it, and it is never the only thing making the action safe.

Relevant classes
aria-disabled
Never the disabled attribute for an action that is not available yet: a disabled button leaves the tab order, so a person who cannot proceed finds nothing there and no explanation of why.
Visible reason
The requirement goes in a .ds-hint under the button that aria-describedby points at — visible, so a sighted person who cannot proceed gets the reason too, and in the accessibility tree, so it is announced when focus lands on the button. Not a hover tooltip: a tooltip is absent from the accessibility tree at exactly the moment nobody is pointing at it.
Disabled fill
.ds-btn:disabled, .ds-btn[aria-disabled="true"] in the shared stylesheet — one rule for every variant, because the message is “you cannot use this”, which does not also need to encode which button it was. The two selectors render identically on purpose: the difference between them is behavioural, and the reader has no use for it.
In flight
Keep the same button in the same place — replacing it with a message throws away the keyboard focus sitting on it. The button is working, not unavailable, so it carries aria-busy rather than aria-disabled and keeps its live rendering; the handler is what ignores a second press. The label is the state, so it changes in the markup and not only in CSS — a spinner alone announces nothing. Under prefers-reduced-motion: reduce the ring stops turning and the label carries the state on its own (DESIGN-POLICIES, Motion).
Destructive
Deleting is not the page’s call to action, so it does not take the primary tier and it does not sit next to the button people press without reading: color and position each carry part of the warning, and the delete goes to the far end of the row, away from Save. Built like the rest of the public set with one departure — hover holds the fill and lightens the inner vignette instead of brightening the color, because a lighter red drops white text below AA. White on Danger Red #c62828 stays at 5.62:1 through every state.
<!-- Not available yet: aria-disabled, with the reason visible and described -->
<button class="ds-btn ds-btn-primary" type="submit"
        aria-disabled="true" aria-describedby="gate">Continue</button>
<p class="ds-hint" id="gate">Grant the license before continuing.</p>

Rows in a repeatable group

Relevant classes
Add
A repeatable group grows on demand — arXiv’s own is “Add top-level file” on the upload page. A real action, but a minor one beside whatever the form is for, so it takes the text tier and sits directly under the group it extends.
Remove
The inverse control belongs inside the row it removes, not in the form’s action row: it acts on one item, not on the form. Text tier again, and the accessible name says which row it removes — a column of buttons all reading “Remove” is a list of identical choices to anyone reading them one at a time. .is-sr-only carries the row name.

Accessibility notes

  • Contrast. Repository Brown on Open Blue #a5d6fe and Repository Brown on Access Lime #c4d82e both pass AA for normal text. White on danger #c62828 is 5.62:1 ✓. Bottom border-gradient hover stops pass the 3:1 UI-component minimum (3.89 / 3.61); the rest-state stops sit below it and are decorative — the button's fill and border carry the boundary at rest.
  • Focus. :focus-visible, never :focus — the ring shows on keyboard nav only.
  • Not color alone. Hover changes fill AND border AND vignette; press changes position AND shadow (WCAG 1.4.1).
  • Target size. Standard padding yields ~37px-tall buttons — above the 24×24 hard floor (WCAG 2.2 SC 2.5.8); aim for 44×44 on touch-primary surfaces.
  • Reduced motion. The only motion is the 1px press drop; wrap transitions in @media (prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference) where a stronger guarantee is needed.
  • Semantics. Real <button> for actions, <a class="ds-btn …"> for navigation. Disabled controls keep cursor: not-allowed.
