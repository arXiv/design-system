Forms & validation

How arXiv builds a form, and how it tells someone what is wrong with one. Below: field construction, the three dispositions a validation result can take, how a problem is surfaced beside its field and again above the form, what every field owes a screen reader, and how the messages should sound. Field widths are set by the type of control, on the assumption that the element already tells you how much someone will type — a textarea holds paragraphs, a date input holds eight characters — and two classes override that where the content is unusually short or unusually long. .ds-field lives in both stylesheets, so the same markup works in internal tools and on the public site — only the accent color differs.

Demo

A field is a label, a control, optional help text, and a slot for a message. That order never changes. Labels sit above their controls, and related fields group by spacing rather than boxes.

Validation

Fields can be in three possible states: normal, validated, or disabled. Validation styles change based on the error disposition — that is, its severity.

ErrorNoThe value is not acceptable and must be corrected.

The info disposition is what makes automatic correction legitimate: normalising whitespace or stripping markup is an edit to author-submitted content, and what makes that acceptable is that the author sees the change before it is committed. The review step is not a nicety to drop later for “obvious” fixes.

Alerts above the form

On a form of any length, repeat the problems above the first field. This is not a new component — it is .ds-alert, specified on its own page, holding an ordered list of links. What follows is only what is particular to using one for validation.

  • One alert per severity, never a mixed one. Red has to mean “you cannot proceed,” and a non-blocking item inside a red alert destroys that. Two problems of two severities means two alerts, in severity order.
  • The heading carries the count, and the body says what that severity means for the reader — whether they may continue.
  • Every row names its field and links to it. Rows repeat the field name even when several name the same field: without it the reader cannot tell whether three problems mean one field to visit or three.
  • Derive every count and every row from the field results. A summary written by hand can disagree with the fields it describes, and eventually will.

2 blocking errors

Blocking errors must be corrected before you can continue. Select an item to jump to that field.

  1. Abstract — do not begin with the word “Abstract”
  2. Abstract — remove the HTML markup <br>

A warning summary is the same construction in .ds-alert-warning, and an automatic change is .ds-alert-info. See Alerts for the four severities and their markup.

Tone

The general rules — no contractions, one instruction per sentence, the same word for the same thing every time, no idiom in text someone reads while something is going wrong — live in STYLE.md and are not repeated here. What follows is specific to validation messages.

Error
Say what to do, not what went wrong. Never blame the reader, never use invalid on its own, and never make someone guess which of eight rules they broke.
Warning
Say what happens if they continue, because they can. “Your submission may be held for moderator review” is the message; “check this field” is not.
Info
Say what changed and ask them to confirm it. This message exists because arXiv edited the author’s content, so it reports rather than instructs, and it never implies fault.

Accessibility essentials

  • A real <label for> on every control, visible or sr-only.
  • required and aria-required="true" on anything mandatory. Required fields carry no visual marker, so this pair is the only signal — it is not optional.
  • Every message needs an id, and its control needs aria-describedby pointing at it. Without that a screen reader announces the field and nothing else.
  • aria-invalid="true" on the control for the error tier only. A warning is not invalid, and a note is not a problem at all.
  • Every tier states itself in text as well as colour — a visually hidden “Error:” or “Warning:” prefix, or the word in the message. Colour and a border are never the only signal (WCAG 1.4.1).
  • Prefer aria-disabled over disabled for a control that could explain itself. disabled leaves the tab order entirely, so a user who cannot proceed finds nothing there and no reason why. The two must look identical.

Easy to get wrong Setting display: flex on an <li> replaces display: list-item and the marker silently disappears — taking the list semantics with it in some screen readers, so the count is never announced. And aria-describedby must point at something present in the accessibility tree: a tooltip toggled with hidden leaves the control undescribed exactly when it is not being hovered. Keep the description in a visually hidden element, written from the same string.

Internal variant

Internal forms mark a required field with an asterisk: <span class="field-required">*</span> inside the <label>, rendered in --ds-danger. Public forms do not, which is why required and aria-required are the only signal there.

The difference is who is filling the form in. A moderator works through the same six screens all day and reads the asterisk as a glance-level scan of what is left; an author meets the submission form a handful of times a year, and a form scattered with asterisks reads as a list of hurdles. The pair of attributes is still required in both places — the asterisk is an addition, never a replacement.

The flow is the same on both surfaces Validate on submit, not on the first keystroke, so nobody is told they are wrong while still typing. Once a field is invalid, re-check it on every input event so the error clears the moment it is fixed. On failure, move focus to the first invalid field.

Layout

  • Labels go above the control. A label-left, field-right layout gives a ragged edge, reads badly on a phone, and makes it easy to ship a control with no label at all.
  • Width follows the content. A field the width of a postcode says what is expected before anyone types. Stretching every control edge-to-edge throws that clue away. Most of this happens without asking: the stylesheet sizes a field from its input type, so a date, a time or a number is already narrow. Two overrides exist for the case the element cannot infer — type="text" covers a four-digit year and a paper title alike. .ds-field--short caps a field at 11rem, for a code, an identifier or a year; .ds-field--full releases the cap for something that genuinely wants the whole measure.
  • Group with spacing and a small heading, not boxes or borders. If spacing cannot hold two groups apart, they probably want to be separate pages.
  • The action bar is right-aligned, primary last. On a long form, repeat it at the top so nobody has to scroll to save.
  • Disabled, not missing. An action that is unavailable renders disabled, so the reader can see it exists and reason about why.

Internal variant

Internal screens are dense enough to need field widths chosen rather than inferred, so the internal stylesheet adds three: .ds-field--sm caps a control at 240px, .ds-field--md at 360px, .ds-field--lg at 480px. The public rule above still holds — the width follows the content — but on an internal form a person picks the size instead of the input type picking it.

Not yet reconciled Two width systems for one idea is one too many. They should become one family the next time the field component is opened; until then, use the pair on public pages and the trio on internal pages, and do not mix them on one form.

Switch

A boolean that applies the moment it is flipped. If the change needs a Save step, it is a checkbox in a form and not this — that is the whole distinction, and it is about when the change lands rather than how the control is built.

Markup

<label class="ds-switch">
  <input type="checkbox" role="switch">
  <span class="ds-switch-track"><span class="ds-switch-thumb"></span></span>
  <span class="ds-switch-label">Email digest</span>
</label>
  • Why role="switch". Without it a screen reader announces “checkbox, checked”. With it, “on” and “off” — which is what the control actually means. It goes on a real checkbox input, so the keyboard behaviour, the state and the form value all stay native and it works with no JavaScript.
  • Why the on-track is not the accent. Open Blue is a light fill built to carry dark text, and a white thumb on it is 1.5:1. --ds-switch-on is the darker end of the accent instead: 6.8:1 against the thumb and 6.4:1 against the page, which is what a UI component boundary needs. The internal stylesheet re-points that one token and changes nothing else.
  • State is not carried by colour alone. The thumb moves, which is a positional cue that survives forced-colors and works for a reader who cannot separate the two track colours (WCAG 1.4.1).
  • The label never changes weight. It is 600 on and off. A bolder face is a wider face — the label grew 1.7px on toggle and shoved every control after it along the row, which is visible in a row of three. The general rule: never signal state with anything that changes text metrics — weight, size, family, letter-spacing. Colour is free.
  • The visible label goes inside the <label>, which is what names the control. A switch with only an adjacent heading has no accessible name.

Internal variant

On the internal surface the switch is the same component with a different on-track. The internal stylesheet re-points one token — --ds-switch-on — and nothing else changes: no second copy of the switch, no override of its parts.

Why this is not just “the green one” Access Lime itself is 1.5:1 against a white thumb, so the on-track takes the darker end of the internal accent, exactly as the public one takes the darker end of Open Blue. The rule is the contrast, not the hue — which is why the token is named for its job.

Segmented control

Markup

<fieldset class="ds-seg">
  <legend class="is-sr-only">Endorsement decision</legend>
  <button type="button" class="ds-seg-btn ds-seg-btn--positive is-active">Approve</button>
  <button type="button" class="ds-seg-btn ds-seg-btn--neutral">Defer</button>
  <button type="button" class="ds-seg-btn ds-seg-btn--negative">Reject</button>
</fieldset>
  • The legend is not optional. A <fieldset> without a <legend> is a group with no accessible name, so a screen reader announces three buttons and never says what they decide. Hide it with .is-sr-only when the surrounding text already says it — hide it, do not omit it.
  • The three colours mean the same thing they mean everywhere else. --positive takes the success tokens, --neutral the info tokens, --negative the error tokens. Accept, informational, reject. They are not free colours to pick from, and they flip for dark mode with the rest of the status palette.
  • Nothing is selected until something is selected. The fourth example above is the resting state of a decision nobody has made yet, and it is a real state — not a bug to hide by pre-selecting the middle option.
  • Buttons, not a radio group, on purpose. A radio group is right when the choice is part of a form that gets submitted. This is for a decision that acts when clicked, which is why .is-active is set by script rather than by :checked.

Tooltip

A short explanation attached to a control, on hover and on focus. The lighter half of the popover family — no title, no close button, and never content that exists nowhere else.

If the paper has been published, the publisher's DOI. Leave it empty otherwise — arXiv mints its own.
Anchored at the trailing edge With .ds-tooltip--end the bubble runs back toward the start, so a control near the trailing edge does not push it off screen.

Hover or tab to either question mark.

  • It opens downward, and toward the inline end. Down, because upward collides with sticky chrome near the top of a page. Toward the end, because that is where a form label sits — at the start of the line, with room to its right. A host near the trailing edge of its container adds .ds-tooltip--end and the bubble runs back the other way. Both use logical properties, so both are correct in a right-to-left script.
  • WCAG 1.4.13 is the whole design, and it is what most hover bubbles fail. Hoverable: the gap between control and bubble is the tooltip's own padding, not a margin, so a pointer moving into it never crosses dead space and loses it. Persistent: it stays while hovered or focused, on no timer. Dismissible: Escape closes it without moving focus — the one part CSS cannot do, so it needs a listener.
  • It opens downward. Upward collides with sticky chrome near the top of a page, and raising z-index hides that rather than fixing it.
  • Describe, never name. The host carries aria-describedby; the tooltip is never the control's accessible name. A name that only appears on hover is a name most people never get — give the control an .is-sr-only label of its own, as the demo does.
  • The trigger never goes inside the <label>. A <label> forwards clicks to the control it names, so a button nested in one is a button whose clicks land somewhere else. Put the label and the trigger side by side.
  • Not for anything required. If the reader must have it to fill the field in, it is hint text under the label, not a tooltip. A tooltip is for the person who stops and wonders.

Stepping through a set

Moving through items one at a time — a moderation queue, a run of submissions — where each item has its own screen and someone works through them in order.

Request 1 of 15

Markup

<nav class="ds-pagination" aria-label="Queue navigation">
  <button class="ds-btn ds-btn-secondary" disabled><svg …></svg> Previous</button>
  <span class="ds-pagination-position" aria-live="polite">Request 1 of 15</span>
  <button class="ds-btn ds-btn-secondary">Next <svg …></svg></button>
</nav>
  • The position is a live region. Without aria-live="polite" a reader who cannot see the counter has no way to know that the Next they just pressed did anything — the page changed and nothing said so.
  • The ends disable, they do not disappear. A control that vanishes at the first item changes the shape of the toolbar under the reader; a disabled one says “there is nothing before this”, which is the actual information.
  • The buttons do not move as you step. The counter takes the slack and centres in it, so going from 9 to 10 grows the text inward rather than shoving the controls either side — the same rule as the switch label: never let a state change alter layout. A min-width cannot do this, because the caller chooses the wording and the component cannot know how wide it gets.
  • Not numbered pages. Nothing at arXiv needs those yet. A stepper answers “what is next”; a row of page numbers answers “take me to item 40”. If a surface ever needs the second, it is a different component and not a variant of this one.
