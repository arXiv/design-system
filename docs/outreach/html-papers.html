Accessibility and usability on HTML papers

How far can we push accessibility and usability on our HTML paper pages? This page is the running answer: what the Phase 1 mockup already demonstrates, and what we are reaching for next.

Shamsi Brinn and Deyan Ginev · drafted 2026-07-30 · published 2026-08-24

Accessibility

  • LaTeXML keeps improving, and everyone benefits at once. The converter is the single biggest lever for accessible science: every release upgrades the rendering of the whole corpus, not one paper at a time.
  • Math in every format, within easy reach. Rendered math, the TeX source, and a copy button sit together. Native MathML with Intent annotations is preserved, so assistive technology reads the mathematics itself, not a picture of it.
  • Alt text made visible. Figure descriptions are available to everyone, with more than one way for screen readers to reach them. Where a description is missing, the page points to our best-practice guides instead of staying silent.
  • Permalinks for the parts of a paper. Sections, headings, formulas, and figures each carry a durable link, so a colleague or a citation can point at the exact spot rather than the whole paper.
  • The abstract travels with the paper. Screen reader users do not need a second trip to the abstract page: the metadata is present where the paper is.
  • Announced correctly. The arXiv identifier is spelled out accurately by screen readers, and arXiv’s own name is announced as it should sound.
  • Gentle nudges. An accessibility panel and alt-text prompts. Subtle, but a start.
  • WCAG 2.1 AA is our basement, not our ceiling. Legal compliance is the floor; we build toward WCAG 2.2 AA.
  • Focus stays where it belongs. Keyboard and screen reader focus is managed inside formula blocks and citation popovers, so exploring one does not mean losing your place.

Usability

  • Versions, clearly. A clean, reusable pattern for displaying a paper’s versions and moving between them.
  • Print conventions, HTML powers. The typographic conventions readers trust from print, plus the features only HTML can offer. The watermark echoes the printed page.
  • Minimal chrome. The “test” and “experimental” messages are gone. As the reader reaches the paper itself, the header shrinks to almost nothing — and stays within reach.
  • Easy paper navigation. A contents toggle for moving through the document, and an obvious route back to the abstract page.
  • Citations, in-house and one action away. The former Bib Explorer is now part of the page, with more functionality and an easy copy.
  • One link that has it all. Paper, metadata, and tools share a single shareable address.
  • Built for phones, math included. Tap-to-activate replaces hover on figures and formulas, and everything wraps — even the equations.
  • References without the jumping around. Popovers show a citation in place; clear navigation is there for when you do want to follow it.
  • Private by construction. Self-hosted fonts and scripts mean fewer dependencies and no trackers — a difference we have measured against the rest of the publishing ecosystem.

What might the future hold?

Directions under discussion — an invitation, not a roadmap.

  • Pinpoint citation. A permanent link and a short written locator — arXiv:2604.22725v1, §2 ¶3 — for every paragraph of a paper, giving citations something more precise than a page number and more durable than a format.
  • Active education around math intent and the other ways authors can make paper content more future-proof, machine readable, and accessible.
  • Community-contributed alt descriptions for images.
  • LaTeXML continues to push the envelope, raising the share of papers that convert fully and faithfully.
  • More papers convertible to HTML: encouraging TeX best practices, discouraging PDF-only submission, and exploring what other source formats could join them.
