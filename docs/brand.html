Brand statement

The statement in full. This is the text under discussion, not a summary of it.

arXiv is the light side of the internet. We were built in 1991 to break the hold commercial publishing had on science. It was too slow, too expensive, too much gatekeeping. We aim to be the opposite and offer the most useful website on the internet, best-in-class for speed, readability, accessibility, and efficiency. The rebellion succeeded, but we still play by its rules: always free, answerable only to researchers, zero ego, and no dark patterns. The light side, indeed.

Voice

Honest, plainspoken, accessible, and slightly mischievous.

  • Honest — We say what is true, including the unflattering parts: open issues, known limitations, “this is a prototype”. No marketing gloss, no overclaiming.
  • Plainspoken — Write as real people, for real people. Our users live around the world with varying familiarity with English, so we avoid slang, contractions and jargon. When we are unsure, Simplified Technical English is the guide.
  • Accessible — Literally (WCAG, screen readers, low bandwidth, JavaScript off) and tonally. Nobody should feel they need a secret handshake to use arXiv.
  • Slightly mischievous — A dry wit, a wink, the bones illustrations. Never cute for its own sake, and never at the reader's expense. Just enough humour to signal there are humans here.

The voice applies to everything that has words: interface copy, empty states, error messages, docs, alt text, commit messages. When in doubt, read it aloud. If it sounds like a brochure or a legal notice, rewrite it. Text that supports a task — error messages, form help, instructions — follows the stricter rules in STYLE.md.

What this means for the design

The brand is not decoration. Each principle names the policy or pattern it drives, so the line from brand to pixel is traceable.

1. The interface gets out of the way
Zero ego means the design never competes with the science. Chrome recedes as the reader goes deeper; navigation stays permanently short and resists link-creep (DESIGN-POLICIES, nav cap). The most useful website is the one you stop noticing.
2. No metrics, no gamification, no dark patterns
Answering only to researchers is literal: arXiv shows no view counts, download counts or citation counts on public pages, and does not rank or promote papers (DESIGN-POLICIES, no metrics display). We do not manufacture urgency, we do not farm attention, and toasts stay near zero. If a feature's job is to drive engagement rather than serve a research task, it does not belong.
3. Lighter than the establishment
The light-side metaphor is in the palette: warm Repository Brown and light tints where commercial publishers go navy-and-corporate. Open Blue is bright, not sombre. The visual lightness is the positioning — see color-mapping.md.
4. Readable by people and machines, equally
Best-in-class readability includes machines: clean semantic HTML, real headings and landmarks, content present before JavaScript runs. Co-equal HTML and PDF serve the same goal. Open strategic question: whether to move from co-equal to HTML-first. A brand decision waiting on leadership, not a design-system one.
5. Accessibility is a brand value, not a checkbox
“The light side” and “open to all” mean WCAG 2.1 AA is the legal floor (the Accessible Canada Act) and 2.2 AA is what we build toward: spoken math, keyboard-reachable everything, forced-colors and reduced-motion support, usability with JavaScript off. The accessibility rules in DESIGN-POLICIES are this principle made concrete.
6. Honest, plainspoken copy — with a wink
Interface text is direct and never overclaims. The mischief lives in small, optional places — the bones illustrations, a dry empty state — never in anything load-bearing, and never in anything a stressed researcher has to parse.
7. Built to last by a small team
arXiv is daily infrastructure maintained by a very small team, so designs must be low-maintenance: no patterns needing manual upkeep, no per-paper hand-tuning, citation formats generated from metadata. Durable and boring-where-it-counts beats clever and fragile. This too is zero ego.
8. Speed and efficiency are different promises
Two distinct pillars, not synonyms. Speed is how fast a paper moves from submission to announced — a throughput commitment. Efficiency is how few clicks a researcher needs to get what they came for. Measured against seventeen other platforms in the clicks-to-content audit, arXiv does not lead here: nearly every open-access publisher puts the full paper on the landing page, while arXiv sends the reader to a separate /html/ page. The directive that follows: count the clicks to every core task — download, cite, find the DOI, switch version — and minimise them. The common cases belong on the surface, not in menus.
